## Key Points

- Relationship reliability is used as a criterion for evaluating differentiable hydrologic parameter learning.
- Distributional learning yields more stable basin attribute-parameter relationships than point-estimate and dropout baselines.
- Learned HBV parameter means and parameter uncertainties show organized, but cautiously interpreted, environmental gradients.

## Abstract

Differentiable parameter learning offers a way to regionalize conceptual hydrologic model parameters with neural networks while retaining a process-based rainfall-runoff backbone. Yet high streamflow skill does not by itself show that learned parameters are stable enough to support hydrologic interpretation. We compare deterministic, Monte Carlo dropout, and distributional formulations for learning static HBV parameters from CAMELS-US basin attributes. Predictive performance is used as a sanity check; the primary evaluation targets the reproducibility and organization of basin attribute-parameter relationships across random seeds, loss functions, and model formulations. All three formulations produced usable simulations, with similar median NSE and KGE across the 531-basin test set. The distributional formulation did not provide a uniform predictive advantage, but it produced the most stable relationship structure, with lower cross-seed and cross-loss variability in Spearman attribute-parameter correlations. The three formulations recovered a shared dominant-control core for seven of fourteen HBV parameters, including slope controls on runoff nonlinearity, potential evapotranspiration controls on storage capacity, aridity controls on percolation, and soil conductivity controls on upper-zone behavior. Distributional parameter means further showed coherent gradients along snow, aridity, terrain, evapotranspiration, and soil axes. Parameter uncertainty was also spatially and environmentally structured, although several uncertainty gradients were mean-coupled or boundary-sensitive. These results suggest that distributional parameter learning is most useful not as a guaranteed route to higher streamflow accuracy, but as a more reliable basis for interpreting regionalized conceptual model parameters and their uncertainty.

## Plain Language Summary

Hydrologic models contain parameters that summarize processes such as snow storage, soil water storage, runoff generation, groundwater exchange, and routing. Modern differentiable models can learn these parameters from basin attributes, but the resulting parameters are not automatically meaningful. This study asks whether the relationships between basin properties and learned HBV parameters are stable enough to interpret. We compare three learning strategies across CAMELS-US basins. The distributional strategy does not clearly outperform the others in streamflow prediction, but it more consistently recovers the same attribute-parameter relationships across training choices. It also provides parameter uncertainty fields that vary systematically across basin environments. The results help separate robust regional controls from relationships that are sensitive to training choices or model compensation.

# 1 Introduction

Differentiable hydrologic modeling has created a practical route for linking process-based model structures with large-sample machine learning. Instead of calibrating each basin independently, a neural network can map static basin attributes to the parameters of a differentiable conceptual rainfall-runoff model. The hydrologic model remains inside the computational graph, so the parameterization can be trained directly against streamflow while retaining process-related states, fluxes, and parameters. This idea is central to recent differentiable, learnable process-based hydrologic models, which have shown that neural regionalization can approach strong predictive skill while preserving a model structure that is more interpretable than a purely black-box sequence model (Feng et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2023).

The interpretability of learned parameters, however, depends on more than hydrograph accuracy. If learned conceptual parameters are used for regionalization, process diagnosis, or ungauged-basin reasoning, the inferred relationships between basin attributes and parameters should be stable under reasonable perturbations of the training procedure. This condition is not guaranteed. Conceptual model parameters are affected by equifinality, process compensation, parameter bounds, calibration objectives, and data limitations (Beven & Freer, 2001). A neural parameterization can fit streamflow adequately while producing attribute-parameter relationships that vary across random seeds or loss functions. Predictive metrics such as NSE or KGE are therefore necessary but insufficient when the scientific objective is to interpret what the learned parameter field represents.

Basin attribute-parameter relationships have long been central to hydrologic regionalization. Regionalization methods assume that climate, topography, soil, vegetation, and geology descriptors contain information about model parameters (Samaniego et al., 2010). Large-sample data sets such as CAMELS-US make it possible to evaluate these relationships over diverse hydroclimatic and physiographic settings (Addor et al., 2017; Newman et al., 2015). Yet the reliability of attribute-parameter relationships under modern differentiable parameter learning remains less studied than predictive performance. In particular, it is unclear whether different neural formulations recover the same dominant controls, whether those controls persist across loss functions and initialization seeds, and whether learned parameter uncertainty contains interpretable basin-scale structure.

This study evaluates three formulations for learning static HBV parameters from CAMELS-US basin attributes. The deterministic formulation maps attributes to a single parameter vector and serves as a point-estimate baseline. The Monte Carlo dropout formulation retains dropout sampling at inference and provides an implicit stochastic baseline. The distributional formulation predicts an attribute-conditioned parameter distribution, yielding both parameter means and parameter-scale standard deviations. We do not treat the distributional formulation as a complete Bayesian posterior. Instead, we ask whether this explicit distributional parameterization produces a more stable and more useful learned parameter field for regional hydrologic interpretation.

The central concept is relationship reliability. We define relationship reliability as the reproducibility of basin attribute-parameter relationships across training perturbations and across alternative learning formulations. This definition deliberately separates several questions that are often conflated. First, do the learned parameters produce credible streamflow simulations? Second, are the parameter values and attribute-parameter correlations stable across random seeds and loss functions? Third, do different formulations recover a shared relationship structure, or do they imply different basin controls? Fourth, once a reliable formulation is identified, do the learned parameter means and uncertainties vary coherently along recognizable environmental gradients? These questions form the evidence chain of the paper.

The contribution is therefore methodological and hydrologic. Methodologically, we show how relationship reliability can be used alongside predictive metrics to evaluate differentiable parameter learning. Hydrologically, we identify a shared set of basin controls on learned HBV parameters and examine how distributional parameter means and uncertainties vary across snow, aridity, topographic, evapotranspiration, and soil gradients. The interpretation is intentionally conservative. We do not claim that learned HBV parameters are direct measurements of physical properties. We instead ask whether the learned behavioral parameter field is coherent, reproducible, and organized by basin attributes in ways that support cautious hydrologic reasoning.

The study also addresses a practical tension in large-sample hydrology. Neural regionalization makes it easy to fit many basins simultaneously, but it can also obscure how the fitted parameter field should be evaluated. A visually plausible map or a statistically significant correlation may still be unstable if it changes with a different seed, objective function, or uncertainty formulation. Conversely, a stable relationship may be scientifically useful even when it is not the largest correlation in the matrix. We therefore organize the manuscript around evidence strength rather than around a single best-performing model. Predictive adequacy, raw parameter stability, relationship stability, cross-formulation agreement, environmental gradients, and uncertainty diagnostics each answer different questions. Only relationships supported by several of these layers are used for the strongest interpretation.

This distinction matters for the role of hybrid hydrologic models. In an operational forecasting setting, a model may be useful if it improves discharge prediction. In a scientific regionalization setting, a learned parameterization is also expected to say something about how basins differ. A relationship that appears under one seed or one objective function but disappears under another cannot provide a strong basis for interpretation. Conversely, a relationship that is moderately strong but stable across seeds, losses, and formulations may be more scientifically useful than a larger but unstable correlation. The present study therefore treats stability as a prerequisite for interpreting learned parameters, not as an optional diagnostic.

The manuscript is structured to reflect this logic. The Methods define the experimental perturbations and relationship-reliability metrics before any hydrologic interpretation is attempted. The Results first establish predictive adequacy, then evaluate relationship stability, then examine shared controls across formulations, and only then interpret environmental gradients in distributional means and uncertainties. The Discussion returns to the difference between predictive usefulness and interpretive reliability, emphasizing where learned HBV parameters can support regional hydrologic reasoning and where compensation, collinearity, or parameter bounds require caution.

The resulting scope is intentionally narrower than a general claim about neural hydrologic modeling. We do not ask whether differentiable HBV is the best possible rainfall-runoff model, nor whether CAMELS-US contains all controls needed for ungauged prediction. We ask a more specific question: when a differentiable conceptual model learns parameters from basin attributes, which parts of the learned parameter field are stable enough to interpret? This narrower framing makes the evidence testable with the available simulations and avoids using predictive success as a proxy for hydrologic meaning.

# 2 Materials and Methods

## 2.1 Data set, basin attributes, and differentiable HBV model

We used CAMELS-US as the large-sample test bed because it provides daily forcing, streamflow observations, and basin attributes for a broad set of minimally disturbed catchments across the contiguous United States. The analysis used 531 basins with matched forcing, streamflow, attributes, coordinates, and complete learned parameter outputs. The static attributes include hydroclimatic, topographic, vegetation, soil, and geology descriptors. Because these attributes are basin-scale descriptors, the primary relationships examined in this paper are cross-basin relationships, not temporal parameter dynamics.

The hydrologic backbone is an HBV-type conceptual model implemented in a differentiable framework. HBV represents snow accumulation and melt, soil water storage, evapotranspiration limitation, percolation, upper and lower groundwater storage, and routing through a compact set of process-related parameters (Bergstrom, 1995). The analyzed parameter set contains fourteen HBV and routing parameters: `parBETA`, `parFC`, `parLP`, `parPERC`, `parUZL`, `parK0`, `parK1`, `parK2`, `route_a`, `route_b`, `parTT`, `parCFMAX`, `parCFR`, and `parCWH`. Parameters were transformed to conventional or physically allowable bounds during simulation. For interpretation and comparison, many diagnostics were computed on normalized parameter values on the [0, 1] search-space scale, while maps and selected tables also use physical-scale estimates.

We interpret the learned parameters as behavioral parameters of a differentiable HBV model. This is an important limitation and also the reason for the stability analysis. A learned value of field capacity, percolation, or routing shape is not treated as a direct observation of a single physical property. It is a compact representation that helps the HBV structure reproduce hydrographs under the learned regionalization. The scientific question is therefore whether these behavioral parameters form a stable cross-basin field that is organized by basin properties.

## 2.2 Parameter-learning formulations

Three neural parameter-learning formulations were compared within the same differentiable HBV framework. The deterministic formulation maps basin attributes to one fixed parameter vector for each basin. It provides a point-estimate baseline and reflects a common use of neural regionalization in which parameter uncertainty is not explicitly represented. The Monte Carlo dropout formulation uses a similar attribute-to-parameter mapping but retains dropout at inference. Repeated stochastic forward passes generate parameter samples, which provide an implicit uncertainty proxy. The distributional formulation predicts the location and scale of an attribute-conditioned parameter distribution. Samples are drawn through reparameterization and then transformed to the HBV parameter ranges, producing both a parameter mean and a parameter standard deviation for each basin and parameter.

The three formulations are not interpreted as competing hydrologic model structures. They share the same HBV backbone and attribute inputs; the comparison targets how the parameter-learning formulation affects reproducibility and interpretability. The dropout formulation is included because dropout is often used as an approximate Bayesian uncertainty mechanism in neural networks (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016), but its parameter spread is not necessarily a clean basin-conditioned parameter distribution. The distributional formulation is included because it explicitly learns parameter-scale uncertainty. We use that uncertainty as a structured diagnostic output, while avoiding the stronger claim that it is a fully calibrated posterior distribution.

## 2.3 Training design and predictive evaluation

The experiment used three loss functions and five random seeds for each formulation, producing 45 complete model runs. The loss functions included an NSE-oriented objective, a logarithmic low-flow-sensitive objective, and a hybrid objective. This design was chosen to separate robust relationships from artifacts of a single initialization or objective function. Each complete run contained parameter estimates for 531 basins and 14 parameters. Existing raw parameter outputs contained duplicate logical rows for some run-basin-parameter keys; the analysis pipeline diagnosed these duplicates and collapsed them by averaging within each model, loss, seed, basin, and parameter. This duplicate handling is disclosed because it affects exact values, although the main rank-based Spearman relationship analyses are less sensitive to parallel duplicate output families than to missing runs.

Predictive performance was summarized using NSE, KGE, absolute bias, and absolute percent bias (Nash & Sutcliffe, 1970; Gupta et al., 2009). These metrics were used as a sanity check rather than the model-selection criterion. The purpose was to verify that the learned parameterizations produced credible streamflow simulations before interpreting the parameter fields. We therefore did not select the preferred formulation by the highest median NSE or KGE. Instead, the subsequent analyses ask whether the learned relationships are stable and hydrologically organized.

The training design deliberately keeps prediction and interpretation separate. Each formulation was evaluated under the same basin set, parameter bounds, loss-function family, and seed set, so differences in relationship reliability can be attributed to the parameter-learning formulation rather than to a different data inventory. Loss functions were retained as structured perturbations rather than pooled as independent experiments. This matters because a loss function can emphasize high flows, low flows, or aggregate hydrograph shape, and these emphases can shift which HBV parameters compensate for errors. A relationship that remains recognizable across these objectives is therefore stronger evidence than a relationship observed under only one objective.

## 2.4 Relationship reliability and model-consistency metrics

For each trained model, we computed Spearman rank correlations between basin attributes and learned HBV parameter values across basins. Spearman correlation is the primary relationship metric because many attribute-parameter relationships are monotonic but not necessarily linear. Pearson and Kendall correlations were used as supporting checks in the analysis workflow, but the main manuscript emphasizes Spearman results. Relationship reliability was summarized with cross-seed variability, cross-loss variability, sign consistency, dominant-attribute consistency, and top-k relationship retention.

Cross-seed variability was computed within each model and loss by comparing attribute-parameter correlations across the five random seeds. Cross-loss variability was computed after seed aggregation, comparing correlation structures across the three loss functions. Candidate high-magnitude relationships were selected neutrally by averaging absolute Spearman correlations across all models, losses, and seeds, not by selecting pairs that favored the distributional formulation. This avoids circular evidence when comparing formulations. Lower seed or loss standard deviation in correlation strength indicates more reproducible relationship recovery. Dominant-control classifications were assigned at the parameter level by comparing the modal dominant attribute and sign across deterministic, dropout, and distributional formulations. Parameters were classified as shared dominant controls, partially shared controls, or model-sensitive controls.

We also compared full attribute-by-parameter correlation matrices. Each model-seed-loss run produced a correlation matrix. Matrix-level similarity was summarized with correlation, cosine similarity, and Frobenius distance. These diagnostics evaluate whether a formulation produces a coherent relationship structure as a whole, rather than only stable individual pairs. A formulation can recover some strong pairwise relationships while still producing a less compact matrix-level structure; both levels are therefore reported.

## 2.5 Environmental gradients, uncertainty diagnostics, and claim safeguards

After the reliability and model-consistency analyses identified the distributional formulation as the primary interpretive formulation, we examined its parameter means and parameter standard deviations along environmental gradients. The main gradients include snow fraction, aridity, potential evapotranspiration, basin slope, soil conductivity, elevation, precipitation seasonality, and selected soil and vegetation attributes. Pairwise correlations were complemented with low, middle, and high tercile group contrasts. Group summaries used medians and interquartile ranges, and high-low contrasts used Mann-Whitney U tests as descriptive evidence of gradient separation.

Distributional parameter uncertainty was analyzed as the learned parameter standard deviation normalized by the corresponding parameter search range. These standard deviations were mapped, correlated with basin attributes, and compared against diagnostics for mean-standard deviation coupling and boundary sensitivity. Boundary sensitivity was important because bounded transformations can create apparent certainty or uncertainty near parameter limits. Mean-standard deviation coupling was important because a standard deviation field may partly track the parameter mean rather than independent identifiability. Uncertainty gradients were therefore treated as structured diagnostic evidence, not as proof of true physical parameter identifiability.

Claims were assigned evidence strength before writing the Results. Strong claims required support from multiple diagnostics, such as seed stability, loss stability, cross-formulation agreement, and coherent environmental gradients. Supportive claims were retained when they were consistent but more sensitive to collinearity, boundary effects, or formulation choice. Cautionary claims were restricted to Discussion or Appendix. This hierarchy is used throughout the manuscript to avoid interpreting every statistically significant correlation as a hydrologic control.

This safeguard is important because the analysis produces many possible attribute-parameter pairs. With hundreds of basins and dozens of attributes, many correlations can be statistically significant without being central to the scientific argument. We therefore treat statistical significance as a screening property, not as sufficient evidence for hydrologic interpretation. Main-text relationships must also have interpretable direction, stability under perturbation, and consistency with the larger matrix or gradient structure. This multi-evidence requirement is intentionally stricter than a conventional correlation table and is designed for the specific problem of interpreting learned conceptual-model parameters.

The final result tables and figures were generated from the same deduplicated analysis tables rather than from separate manual extracts. This was done to keep the evidence chain auditable: predictive metrics, parameter estimates, correlation matrices, stability summaries, environmental-gradient tables, and uncertainty diagnostics all retain model, loss, seed, basin, parameter, and attribute identifiers where relevant. Main figures summarize the subset needed for the argument, while the appendix records the broader inventory, sensitivity checks, and supplemental visualizations. This separation reduces the risk that the main text overfits to isolated attractive panels while still allowing the full set of analysis outputs to be inspected.

For the same reason, figure captions are written as part of the interpretation rather than as a detached inventory. Each main figure is tied to a specific question in the evidence chain, and appendix figures are used to document robustness, alternative formulations, or diagnostic caveats for transparent manuscript revision.

# 3 Results

## 3.1 Predictive performance established adequacy but not model preference

All three formulations produced usable streamflow simulations across the 531 CAMELS-US basins (Figure 1 and Table 1). Median predictive metrics were similar among formulations. Across all losses and seeds, median NSE was 0.611 for the deterministic formulation, 0.624 for Monte Carlo dropout, and 0.611 for the distributional formulation. Median KGE was 0.626, 0.639, and 0.625, respectively. Under the hybrid loss, the three formulations were even closer: median NSE ranged from 0.632 to 0.636 and median KGE from 0.675 to 0.679. These results show that the distributional formulation was not selected because it clearly dominated streamflow prediction.

The predictive comparison instead motivates the relationship-based evaluation. The dropout formulation had the highest overall median NSE and KGE, but it also had slightly larger median absolute bias than the other two formulations. The deterministic and distributional formulations were nearly indistinguishable in their overall median NSE and KGE. Differences by loss were larger than differences among formulations for some metrics, especially between the logarithmic objective and the NSE-oriented objectives. Thus, predictive performance confirms that all three learned parameterizations were credible enough to analyze, but it does not identify a single formulation as scientifically preferable.

Parameter-value stability added a second caution against choosing a formulation by a single metric (Figure 2). Raw cross-seed parameter variability was formulation- and parameter-dependent. Under the hybrid loss, the deterministic formulation had the lowest median normalized seed standard deviation across all parameters, while under the logarithmic loss dropout was slightly lower and under the NSE loss deterministic again appeared lowest. However, this apparent raw stability was confounded by boundary saturation. Several parameters, including `parCWH`, `parCFR`, `parK0`, `route_a`, and `route_b`, were frequently near search-range boundaries, where small numerical variation can reflect boundary locking rather than reliable learned structure. After excluding boundary-sensitive parameters, the separation among formulations became smaller and depended on the loss.

This first result is important because it defines the role of prediction and raw parameter reproducibility in the manuscript. The analyses do not show that distributional learning is a universal predictive improvement, nor do they show that it always gives the smallest raw parameter variance. Instead, they show that all formulations satisfy a performance adequacy condition and that raw parameter stability alone is not a sufficient interpretive criterion. The decisive question is whether the cross-basin relationships between basin attributes and parameters are reproduced consistently across training perturbations.

## 3.2 Relationship stability favored the distributional formulation

Cross-seed relationship stability provided the first strong evidence for the distributional formulation (Figure 3). For high-magnitude candidate relationships selected independently of any single formulation, the median seed standard deviation of Spearman rho was 0.0241 for `delta_dist`, compared with 0.0347 for `delta_mcd` and 0.0461 for `delta_base`. The median seed range in rho followed the same ordering: 0.0630 for `delta_dist`, 0.0891 for `delta_mcd`, and 0.1122 for `delta_base`. Paired nonparametric comparisons confirmed that the distributional formulation had lower seed variability than both baselines for the selected candidate relationship set. The effect sizes were modest in absolute rho units, but the ordering was consistent across seed standard deviation and seed range.

The same conclusion held when the analysis was summarized over the full relationship-sensitivity workflow. Mean seed SD in rho was 0.0308 for `delta_dist`, compared with 0.0663 for `delta_base` and 0.0546 for `delta_mcd` (Table 2). This result is not equivalent to saying that every individual attribute-parameter pair was more stable under the distributional formulation. Some pairs were weak, near-zero, or sensitive to particular losses. Rather, the distributional formulation more reproducibly recovered the relationship structure for the candidate controls that matter most for interpretation.

Dominant-control reproducibility also favored `delta_dist`. Median dominant attribute consistency across seeds was 1.000 for the distributional formulation, compared with 0.800 for both baselines in the Figure 3 candidate analysis. Median top-5 overlap was 0.700 for `delta_dist`, 0.679 for `delta_mcd`, and 0.629 for `delta_base`. Sign consistency was high for all formulations after near-zero relationships were handled cautiously, indicating that the main difference was not simply sign flipping but reproducibility of relationship strength and ranking. Four selected robust pairs, `slope_mean-parBETA`, `pet_mean-parFC`, `aridity-parPERC`, and `soil_conductivity-parUZL`, retained interpretable structure across seeds and became the main examples used later in the hydrologic interpretation.

Loss sensitivity was generally larger than seed sensitivity, which is expected because the loss function changes the hydrograph features emphasized during training. Even under this stronger perturbation, `delta_dist` had the lowest cross-loss relationship variability. Mean cross-loss SD in rho was 0.129 for `delta_dist`, 0.142 for `delta_base`, and 0.168 for `delta_mcd` (Table 2). Cross-loss ranges showed the same ordering. This result supports the statement that distributional learning is more reliable across objective functions, but it should be read as an overall tendency rather than a pairwise guarantee. Some snow and routing relationships remained more loss-sensitive than storage or production relationships, consistent with compensation among HBV process pathways.

Basin-group analyses showed that the relationship-stability result was not driven by a single hydroclimatic regime (Figure 4). Seven externally defined basin groups, each with at least 40 study basins, covered humid steep, low-snow humid lowland, arid lowland, arid seasonal, low-snow arid steep, snow arid steep, and snow humid steep settings. Within these groups, `delta_dist` generally maintained lower seed-to-seed variability in high-correlation relationships. The median group-wise seed SD in rho was 0.0491 for `delta_dist`, compared with 0.0586 for `delta_base` and 0.0541 for `delta_mcd`. Top-5 overlap was not uniformly highest for `delta_dist`, but the overall group analysis indicated that its relationship structure remained controlled across environmental strata. Within-group parameter spread did not indicate uncontrolled dispersion of distributional parameters, although it was not always the smallest among formulations.

Together, these results justify using `delta_dist` as the primary formulation for hydrologic interpretation. The justification is not predictive dominance. It is the more reproducible recovery of cross-basin attribute-parameter relationships across random seeds, loss functions, and basin groups.

This evidence also clarifies why relationship reliability is distinct from statistical significance. With 531 basins, many attribute-parameter correlations can have small p-values, especially when broad climate and physiographic gradients are involved. The stability analyses ask a different question: whether the same relationship is recovered when the training process is perturbed. The distributional formulation performed better on that question. It reduced the variability of correlation strength, improved dominant-control reproducibility, and preserved interpretable examples across the candidate relationship set. These properties make the later hydrologic interpretation more defensible than a simple list of large correlations would be.

## 3.3 The three formulations recovered a shared but uneven dominant-control core

The model-consistency analysis showed that the three formulations did not learn unrelated parameter maps (Figure 5, Figure 6, and Table 3). Seven of the fourteen parameters were classified as shared dominant controls, six as partially shared controls, and one as model-sensitive. The shared group included `parBETA`, `parFC`, `parK2`, `parPERC`, `parTT`, `parUZL`, and `route_a`. For these parameters, the modal dominant attribute matched across deterministic, dropout, and distributional formulations, and the dominant relationship direction was consistent. This shared core is important because it separates robust basin controls from formulation-specific artifacts.

The clearest shared controls were hydrologically interpretable. `parBETA` was consistently linked to mean basin slope, with a negative distributional Spearman correlation of -0.583. `parFC` was consistently linked to mean potential evapotranspiration, with a positive correlation of 0.509. `parPERC` was consistently linked to aridity, with a negative correlation of -0.594. `parUZL` was consistently linked to soil conductivity, with a positive correlation of 0.570. `route_a` was consistently linked to slope, although routing parameters require more cautious interpretation because they can absorb timing and compensation effects. These examples form the strongest hydrologic evidence because they combine cross-formulation agreement, seed stability, and coherent environmental gradients.

The partially shared controls were also informative. `parCFR` and `parCWH`, which are snow-related parameters, were dominated by snow fraction in the dropout and distributional formulations but by slope in the deterministic formulation. Direction consistency remained high, suggesting that the formulations emphasized related environmental structure even when the exact dominant attribute differed. `parCFMAX`, `parK0`, `parK1`, and `route_b` also fell into partially shared classes. `route_b` was especially cautionary because it showed a sign-flip flag in the model-consistency table. Only `parLP` was classified as model-sensitive, with different modal dominant attributes across the three formulations.

Matrix-level similarity supported this interpretation at the structure level. The distributional formulation had the highest within-model compactness, 0.809, compared with 0.702 for `delta_base` and 0.680 for `delta_mcd`. Its mean Frobenius distance among within-model correlation matrices was also lower, 2.624 versus 3.453 and 3.668 for the deterministic and dropout baselines. At the same time, intermodel similarities showed that `delta_dist` remained close to the shared relationship-matrix region. The distributional formulation therefore did not invent a different hydrologic rule set. It expressed a broadly shared relationship structure more compactly and reproducibly.

The distributional parameter maps reinforced this view (Figure 5). Parameter means varied substantially across the 531 basins, with most parameters spanning a large portion of their normalized search-space range. Spatial organization was visible for storage, runoff-generation, recession, routing, and snow parameters. These maps are not independent proof of physical parameter meaning, because no spatial autocorrelation model was fitted and parameter compensation remains possible. They do show, however, that the learned distributional parameter field is not spatially random. The subsequent attribute-gradient analyses explain which basin properties organize that field.

## 3.4 Distributional means and uncertainties followed structured environmental gradients

Distributional parameter means showed strong and interpretable relationships with basin attributes (Figure 6, Figure 8, and Figure 9). The strongest individual relationship was between `parCWH` and snow fraction, with Spearman rho = -0.904. `parCFR` also decreased with snow fraction, rho = -0.625. Among the shared dominant controls, `parPERC` decreased with aridity, rho = -0.594; `parBETA` decreased with slope, rho = -0.583; `parUZL` increased with soil conductivity, rho = 0.570; and `parFC` increased with potential evapotranspiration, rho = 0.509. These values indicate that the distributional parameter means are organized by major environmental gradients rather than by isolated weak correlations.

Tercile group contrasts made these relationships more interpretable. High-snow basins had substantially lower median `parCWH` than low-snow basins, with a high-minus-low normalized median difference of -0.343 and an almost complete rank-biserial separation. High-snow basins also had lower `parCFR`, with a high-minus-low difference of -0.076. High-aridity basins had lower `parPERC`, difference -0.229, and higher `parFC`, difference 0.160 in the selected key-gradient table. High-slope basins had lower `parBETA`, difference -0.403, and higher `parUZL`, difference 0.369. High soil-conductivity basins had higher `parUZL`, difference 0.260. These group contrasts support the heatmap relationships and show that the gradients are visible at the basin-group scale, not only in pairwise rank correlations.

The detailed gradient panels show that several relationships were monotonic but not necessarily linear (Figure 9). This is one reason Spearman correlation was used as the primary measure. Some gradients were sharp over a limited attribute range, whereas others changed gradually across quantile bins. For example, the snow-related parameters showed strong separation between low- and high-snow basins, while storage and production parameters often changed more gradually along aridity, PET, slope, and soil-conductivity axes. This structure is consistent with HBV parameters acting as behavioral summaries of multiple interacting processes rather than as one-to-one mappings from a single attribute.

The uncertainty analyses added a second layer of information (Figure 7, Figure 8, and Figure 10). Parameter standard deviations were not randomly distributed across basins. Some of the strongest uncertainty relationships were clean snow-related gradients: `parCFMAX` uncertainty decreased with snow fraction, rho = -0.919, and `parTT` uncertainty decreased with snow fraction, rho = -0.910. These patterns are consistent with the idea that snow-process parameters are more tightly organized where snow processes are more strongly expressed. Other uncertainty gradients were structured but flagged for caution. `parCWH` uncertainty decreased with snow fraction, rho = -0.922, but was interpreted with caution because of diagnostic flags. `parPERC` uncertainty decreased with aridity, rho = -0.584; `parUZL` uncertainty increased with soil conductivity, rho = 0.578; and `route_b` uncertainty increased with snow fraction, rho = 0.599. Several of these were mean-coupled or boundary-sensitive.

The key point is therefore not that every uncertainty gradient measures physical identifiability. The defensible conclusion is narrower: the learned distributional standard deviations contain structured basin-environment information, and that structure can be diagnosed. Figure 7 separates uncertainty-attribute correlations from mean-standard deviation coupling and boundary effects. This diagnostic separation is essential because a bounded parameter near a limit can appear certain, and a parameter with larger mean values can also have larger absolute standard deviations. The uncertainty field is useful, but it must be interpreted together with these safeguards.

Representative basin cases provide an intuitive synthesis (Figure 11). The selected snow, arid, humid, steep, soil/storage, and routing cases were chosen from basins with at least median distributional predictive performance and extreme attribute percentiles. Their parameter regimes reflect the large-sample gradients: snow-dominated cases show distinct snow-parameter behavior, steep cases show low `parBETA` and high upper-zone structure, and soil-conductivity cases emphasize storage and threshold parameters. These cases are illustrative rather than inferential. They do not prove basin-specific true parameters, but they show that the large-sample relationships translate into recognizable basin-level parameter regimes.

# 4 Discussion

## 4.1 Distributional learning is valuable because it improves interpretability, not because it guarantees higher prediction

The main finding is that distributional differentiable parameter learning changes the type of information that can be extracted from a regionalized conceptual model. It did not uniformly improve median streamflow metrics relative to deterministic or dropout baselines. In fact, the dropout formulation had slightly higher overall median NSE and KGE in the available runs. If the study were framed only as an accuracy benchmark, the distributional formulation would not be the obvious winner. Its advantage appears when the learned parameters are treated as scientific objects that must support reproducible basin relationships.

This distinction is central to differentiable hydrologic modeling. A neural parameterization may use basin attributes effectively for prediction while still producing parameter relationships that are unstable across seeds or losses. Such instability is problematic when the learned parameters are interpreted as regional hydrologic information. The distributional formulation produced the most stable attribute-parameter relationship structure and the most compact matrix-level organization. It also provided a parameter uncertainty field that could be mapped and related to basin attributes. These two features, relationship reliability and explicit uncertainty, are the basis for its interpretive value.

The deterministic formulation remains useful. It is simple, competitive in prediction, and recovered several of the same dominant controls. Its limitation is that it provides only a point estimate and showed stronger sensitivity in relationship structure. The dropout formulation is also useful as a stochastic baseline, but its parameter spread is induced by dropout and was less coherent as a relationship field. The distributional formulation occupies a middle ground. It is not a full Bayesian posterior, but it explicitly predicts basin-conditioned parameter means and scales. That structure makes it easier to evaluate whether uncertainty itself is organized by basin environment.

This interpretation should remain bounded. The distributional standard deviations are learned outputs of a particular neural-HBV training framework. They do not include all sources of uncertainty, such as forcing error, streamflow measurement error, structural error, or posterior uncertainty under a formal likelihood. Their value is diagnostic and comparative: they show where the learned parameter mapping is more or less variable within the adopted formulation. Treating them as calibrated physical uncertainty would overstate the evidence.

## 4.2 Robust controls are behavioral hydrologic signatures, not universal parameter laws

The shared dominant-control core suggests that several HBV parameters are consistently organized by recognizable basin attributes. The negative relationship between `parBETA` and slope links terrain to the runoff-generation nonlinearity represented by HBV. In steeper basins, the learned parameter field favors lower normalized `parBETA`, which may indicate a more responsive soil-runoff partitioning behavior within the model. The positive relationship between `parFC` and potential evapotranspiration suggests that storage-capacity behavior is organized by evaporative demand, although the learned `parFC` should not be equated with measured field capacity. The negative relationship between `parPERC` and aridity indicates that percolation behavior changes systematically across wet-dry gradients. The positive relationship between `parUZL` and soil conductivity is consistent with upper-zone threshold or drainage behavior being linked to soil hydraulic properties.

These interpretations are deliberately phrased as behavioral signatures. HBV parameters summarize interacting processes and compensation pathways. A correlation between a basin attribute and a learned parameter can be hydrologically meaningful without being a direct causal law. Attribute collinearity further complicates interpretation. Aridity, potential evapotranspiration, precipitation seasonality, elevation, snow fraction, and slope are not independent environmental axes in CAMELS-US. A dominant relationship may therefore represent a broader hydroclimatic or physiographic gradient rather than the isolated effect of one attribute.

The snow-related parameters illustrate why the evidence hierarchy is necessary. `parCWH` and `parCFR` showed some of the strongest distributional gradients, especially with snow fraction. These are visually and statistically compelling relationships, but they were only partially shared across formulations. The deterministic model often emphasized slope for these parameters, while dropout and distributional formulations emphasized snow fraction. This pattern does not make the snow gradients unimportant. Instead, it suggests that cold-region information strongly organizes the learned parameter field, but the exact parameter pathway can depend on the learning formulation. The appropriate conclusion is that snow fraction is a major organizing gradient for learned HBV behavior, not that every snow-parameter relationship is a universal physical rule.

Routing and recession parameters require similar caution. `route_a` was classified as a shared dominant control linked to slope, which is hydrologically plausible. `route_b`, however, was partially shared and sign-sensitive. Recession parameters can absorb differences in storage behavior, runoff generation, routing delay, and objective-function emphasis. They are therefore useful indicators of model behavior but weaker evidence for direct basin controls than the most stable storage and production relationships.

## 4.3 Structured uncertainty can support regionalization if diagnostic caveats are retained

Regionalization requires transferring information from gauged to ungauged basins through basin attributes. The present results suggest that the quality of this transfer should not be judged only by discharge prediction. If the parameter-attribute relationships used for transfer are unstable, then good predictive skill in gauged basins does not necessarily translate into reliable scientific interpretation. Relationship reliability is therefore a practical criterion for evaluating differentiable regionalization.

The distributional formulation is especially relevant to regionalization because it provides both a central parameter estimate and a basin-conditioned uncertainty estimate. In principle, this can help identify where a learned parameter relationship is more or less constrained. For example, clean snow-related uncertainty gradients for `parCFMAX` and `parTT` suggest that snow-process parameter spread is systematically organized by snow fraction and elevation-related gradients. Such information could guide ensemble construction, basin selection for additional observations, or comparison among model structures.

At the same time, uncertainty gradients must be interpreted with the diagnostics. Several strong uncertainty relationships were flagged as mean-coupled or boundary-sensitive. A standard deviation can decrease near a parameter bound because the transformation restricts available variation, not because the process is physically well identified. A standard deviation can also increase with the mean if the model represents uncertainty on a scale that is not independent of parameter magnitude. The manuscript therefore treats uncertainty gradients as structured diagnostic signals. They are valuable because they reveal where the learned mapping varies systematically, but they are not standalone proof of identifiability.

This balanced interpretation is useful for future parameter-learning studies. Many regionalization approaches produce a single parameter vector for each basin. Those vectors can be mapped and correlated with attributes, but they do not show where the parameter mapping is more uncertain. A distributional formulation adds an uncertainty layer that can be analyzed using the same large-sample framework as parameter means. Even approximate uncertainty can be useful if its limitations are explicit and if diagnostics distinguish clean gradients from boundary-driven or mean-coupled patterns.

The same logic can inform ungauged-basin applications. In a strict prediction study, the main question is whether a regionalized model predicts discharge at held-out basins. In an interpretive regionalization study, the transferred parameter field should also be explainable in terms of basin properties. If two basins have similar attributes but the learned parameter mapping assigns very different behavioral regimes under different random seeds, then the regionalization is difficult to trust scientifically. Relationship reliability offers a way to detect that problem before the learned parameters are used for inference. It can also identify which parameters are more suitable for interpretation and which should be propagated as uncertain or compensatory elements in an ensemble.

## 4.4 Limitations and future work

Several limitations should guide interpretation. First, the study cannot prove that one formulation recovers true physical parameter values. The claim is narrower: within a differentiable HBV framework trained on CAMELS-US, the distributional formulation recovers more stable and coherent attribute-parameter relationships. Second, the results may depend on the HBV structure, parameter bounds, forcing data, basin attributes, and selected loss functions. The seed and loss perturbations reduce this concern but do not cover all forms of model uncertainty.

Third, the duplicate-row diagnosis means that exact raw parameter values should be interpreted with moderate caution. The analysis collapsed duplicate logical parameter rows before computing relationships, and all complete run inventories were preserved. Because the main evidence is rank-based and relationship-level, this issue is less likely to overturn the qualitative conclusions, but it should be documented in any submitted supplement. Fourth, several attributes are correlated, and no causal identification or partial-correlation framework is used as the main evidence. The results are best read as stable empirical regularities within the learned HBV parameter field.

Fifth, the analysis is cross-basin rather than temporal. This is appropriate because the neural inputs are static basin attributes and the scientific question concerns regional parameter fields. It does not address time-varying parameter behavior, event-scale process switching, or climate nonstationarity. Future work could extend the relationship-reliability framework to dynamic attributes, alternative conceptual models, other CAMELS regions, and transfer tests in ungauged basins. Comparisons with a fuller probabilistic model could also separate parameter uncertainty from forcing, observation, and structural uncertainty.

Despite these limitations, the results support a clear design principle. Differentiable hydrologic parameter learning should be evaluated not only by predictive skill, but also by whether the learned parameter field is stable enough for scientific use. A model that changes its dominant basin controls when the seed or loss function changes is difficult to interpret. A model that recovers a shared relationship core and expresses it coherently across basins can provide useful hydrologic insight even without predictive dominance. The distributional formulation studied here is valuable because it strengthens that interpretive pathway.

The framework can be applied with little dependence on the exact HBV parameter set. A future study could replace HBV with another conceptual model, use different basin descriptors, or evaluate transfer to a different CAMELS region, while keeping the same core questions: Are the simulations adequate? Are relationship matrices stable across seeds and objectives? Are dominant controls shared across plausible formulations? Do parameter means and uncertainties follow interpretable gradients? Which gradients survive boundary and coupling diagnostics? Answering these questions would make learned-parameter interpretation more reproducible and would reduce the risk of building hydrologic narratives around unstable neural artifacts.

# 5 Conclusions

This study compared deterministic, Monte Carlo dropout, and distributional formulations for learning static HBV parameters from basin attributes in a differentiable hydrologic modeling framework. The analysis used 531 CAMELS-US basins, 14 HBV and routing parameters, three loss functions, and five random seeds per formulation. Predictive performance was treated as an adequacy check rather than the primary selection criterion. All formulations produced usable streamflow simulations, and the distributional formulation did not uniformly outperform the baselines in NSE or KGE.

The main conclusion is that the distributional formulation provided the most reliable basis for interpreting learned parameter relationships. It had lower cross-seed and cross-loss variability in basin attribute-parameter Spearman correlations, stronger dominant-control reproducibility, and higher matrix-level compactness than the deterministic and dropout baselines. This relationship-level advantage was more important than raw parameter-value stability, which was affected by boundary saturation and varied by loss and parameter.

The three formulations recovered a shared dominant-control core for seven of fourteen parameters. The most defensible examples linked `parBETA` to basin slope, `parFC` to potential evapotranspiration, `parPERC` to aridity, and `parUZL` to soil conductivity. These relationships were stable, cross-formulation consistent, and visible as distributional environmental gradients. Snow-related parameters showed some of the strongest gradients, especially with snow fraction, but were treated more cautiously because their dominant controls were only partially shared across formulations.

The distributional formulation also revealed structured parameter uncertainty. Some uncertainty gradients, particularly snow-related gradients for `parCFMAX` and `parTT`, were clean diagnostic signals. Other strong gradients were mean-coupled or boundary-sensitive and should not be read as direct identifiability evidence. The uncertainty field is therefore useful when interpreted with diagnostics, not as a complete posterior.

Overall, distributional differentiable parameter learning is most valuable here because it improves the reliability and interpretability of regionalized conceptual parameters, not because it guarantees higher streamflow skill. The relationship-reliability framework provides a practical way to distinguish robust basin controls from formulation-sensitive or compensatory relationships. It also changes how learned parameter uncertainty can be used: not as a definitive physical posterior, but as a structured diagnostic layer that can be mapped, tested against basin attributes, and screened for coupling or boundary artifacts.

The broader implication is that learned hydrologic parameters should be evaluated collectively. A single correlation can be misleading when parameters compensate for one another or when basin attributes are collinear. Confidence increases when a relationship is stable across seeds, robust across losses, shared across formulations, visible in environmental gradients, and supported by uncertainty diagnostics or representative basin profiles. This multi-evidence logic is the main methodological contribution of the study. It can be extended to other conceptual models, other large-sample hydrologic data sets, and future ungauged-basin regionalization experiments where learned parameters are used not only for prediction but also for scientific interpretation.

The hydrologic contribution is similarly bounded but useful. The results identify a small set of learned HBV relationships that are strong enough to guide interpretation, and a second set that should be treated as formulation-dependent or diagnostic. This distinction is the main practical outcome for regionalization. It suggests which learned controls can be discussed with higher confidence, which should motivate further testing, and which should be propagated with caution in future applications. In that sense, the study does not simply prefer one model formulation; it provides a workflow for deciding when learned conceptual parameters are reliable enough to carry scientific meaning.

## Appendix A. Training inventory and data readiness

The analysis used all discovered output directories containing the `531` basin marker. The final run inventory contained 45 complete runs: three model formulations, three loss functions, and five random seeds. The formulations were deterministic (`delta_base`), Monte Carlo dropout (`delta_mcd`), and distributional (`delta_dist`). The loss functions were `HybridNseBatchLoss`, `LogNseBatchLoss`, and `NseBatchLoss`. Seeds were 111, 222, 333, 444, and 555. Each complete parameter run contained 7434 basin-parameter rows after deduplication, corresponding to 531 basins and 14 parameters.

All 531 basins had matched CAMELS attributes and coordinates. The core attribute set used in the main heatmaps contained 35 numeric descriptors spanning hydroclimate, topography/scale, vegetation/land cover, soil, and geology. The distributional spatial map tables contained 531 basins by 14 parameters for parameter means and the same coverage for parameter uncertainty.

Raw parameter source files contained duplicate logical rows for some model/loss/seed/basin/parameter keys. The pipeline explicitly diagnosed these duplicates and collapsed them by averaging within each logical key. This is a moderate caveat for exact raw parameter values, especially for boundary-sensitive parameters. The primary claims are less exposed because they rely on deduplicated run inventories, rank-based cross-basin Spearman relationships, and repeated stability comparisons rather than single raw output rows.

## Appendix B. Model formulations and parameter output definitions

For the deterministic formulation, each basin has one parameter estimate per model run. For the dropout formulation, stochastic inference produces repeated parameter samples. For the distributional formulation, the neural network predicts a location and scale for each parameter before transformation to the HBV search range. Parameter means are computed as the central learned estimates, and parameter standard deviations are normalized by the corresponding parameter range for uncertainty analyses.

The main parameter order is `parBETA`, `parFC`, `parLP`, `parPERC`, `parUZL`, `parK0`, `parK1`, `parK2`, `route_a`, `route_b`, `parTT`, `parCFMAX`, `parCFR`, and `parCWH`. Physical bounds used for mapping and normalization are: `parBETA` 1.0-6.0, `parFC` 50-1000, `parLP` 0.2-1.0, `parPERC` 0-10, `parUZL` 0-100, `parK0` 0.05-0.9, `parK1` 0.01-0.5, `parK2` 0.001-0.2, `route_a` 0-2.9, `route_b` 0-6.5, `parTT` -2.5-2.5, `parCFMAX` 0.5-10, `parCFR` 0-0.1, and `parCWH` 0-0.2.

The uncertainty intervals shown for stochastic formulations are descriptive. For dropout and distributional outputs, q05-q95 widths summarize stochastic parameter sample spread. For distributional uncertainty gradients, the main quantity is parameter standard deviation normalized by the physical search range. These values should not be interpreted as fully calibrated posterior intervals.

## Appendix C. Relationship-reliability workflow

The relationship-reliability analysis used basin-level correlations between static CAMELS attributes and learned parameter values. Correlations were computed across basins, not across time steps. This distinction is important because the basin attributes are static descriptors and the scientific object is the regional organization of learned parameter fields.

The workflow proceeded in four stages. First, parameter estimates were deduplicated and normalized when needed. Second, seed-wise attribute-parameter Spearman matrices were computed for each model, loss, and seed. Third, cross-seed and cross-loss variability were summarized by standard deviation, range, sign consistency, dominant-attribute consistency, and top-k overlap. Fourth, model-consistency classes were assigned by comparing modal dominant attributes and signs across formulations.

Candidate relationships used in the main seed-stability figure were selected by averaging absolute Spearman rho across all formulations, losses, and seeds. This avoids selecting only relationships favorable to the distributional model. Full-pair summaries and top-k sensitivity checks are retained in the supplement because many weak pairs have little hydrologic meaning and can make sign metrics unstable.

## Appendix D. Boundary and uncertainty diagnostics

Boundary diagnostics were necessary because several HBV parameters were frequently near the lower or upper search-range limits. Boundary-sensitive parameters included `parBETA`, `parCFMAX`, `parCFR`, `parCWH`, `parK0`, `parK1`, `parK2`, `parLP`, `route_a`, and `route_b`. Apparent stability for such parameters can reflect boundary locking rather than reliable learning.

For uncertainty interpretation, two diagnostics were used. Mean-standard deviation coupling measures whether parameter standard deviation strongly follows the parameter mean. Boundary distance-standard deviation coupling measures whether uncertainty is largely controlled by proximity to a parameter bound. Parameters with large coupling values were flagged as requiring cautious interpretation. These flags do not invalidate the existence of structured uncertainty gradients; they constrain the claim from physical identifiability to diagnostic structure.

Clean uncertainty-gradient examples include snow-related uncertainty gradients for `parCFMAX` and `parTT`. Cautionary examples include strong gradients for `parCWH`, `parPERC`, `parUZL`, and routing parameters where mean coupling or boundary sensitivity may contribute.
